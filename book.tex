\noindent If you're reading this, then my weird little deck is here in your hands, and you have a question like ``what the hell is going on'' or ``why is it like this''. Let me start at the beginning.

My name is Jes, and I'm an astrolabist, which means I'm concerned with astronomy and astrology through the lens of how people understood them before the modern era: the math and mythology are deeply intertwined, and to get to the good stuff you need to understand a lot of interlocking systems. No particular part on its own is overwhelming, but all together is too much for the human mind to deal with. It is my hope that tarot can be a bridge between these different parts.

Tarot, despite its origins as a simple card game, took on esoteric connotations as various people noticed numerical coincidences in its structure, and gradually reinterpreted it to be a projection of some deep structure of the universe, or the human mind, or aspects of the divine, etc. The Tarot we know today is deeply astrological, as systematic mappings to the planets, zodiac, and ecliptic were used to design the illustrations on the most recognizable historical tarot decks. Many of those associations are obscure now, and one goal of this project is to try to bring them to the forefront.

Many people over thousands of years attempted to systematize the motion of the planets and the stars. They all made slightly different choices, but because they were all referring to the same real objects in the actual sky, similar patterns emerged in all of them. An extraordinary gift of our time is that information for cultures that once had sparse contact with each other are now freely available for anyone to read about. I have selected a few systems of astrology and divination to represent here, from a few of the world's largest cultures --- not because they are the best, but because they are well-documented, and an easy place to start. I lament that it is impossible to know every idea that was ever revered by any culture, but I have done my best to present the little sliver of the world's knowledge that I have managed to understand. I hope you find them as beautiful as I do.

\noindent This book is in two parts, the first part is concerned with the individual systems that appear on the cards, while the second part will examine each card individually, with reference to how each of those systems manifest on a particular card. Big picture, little picture. We'll start with the simplest system possible: duality.

Long before Alan Turing, John von Neumann, and Claude Shannon applied binary encodings to the problem of electronically processing information, masters of divination used binary logic to transform randomness into recognizable symbols. The inputs could be as simple as a coin flip or be composed of laborious rituals that could take the better part of an hour to generate a single figure.

The oldest such system is the I Ching, which originated in the Yellow River valley of China, around 1050 BCE. In this system, the two fundamental bits are yang lines and yin lines, the active and passive elements.

A remarkably similar system developed in West Africa where it is known as Ifá (or Fa, or Afa), and was transmitted to the Arab world where it was called \textit{ilm al-raml} (``the science of sand''), and eventually reached medieval Europe where it became Geomancy. In this system, active and passive elements are marked as single (odd) or paired (even) dots.

We will discuss how binary symbols combine to form more complicated symbols in Chapter 8: Trigrams, Chapter 16: Geomantic Figures, and Chapter 64: Hexagrams.

Even though it's common to associate this polarity with ``male'' and ``female'', I'd caution against over-indexing on that reading. Maybe it's more like sperm and egg: there's a real difference in strategy, but to say that it \textit{is} gender is a dramatic oversimplification of both concepts.

Western and eastern systems disagree somewhat about which planets should be considered active or passive --- in the European tradition, Mercury stands outside the binary, but in the Chinese system, it is Saturn who is ambiguous.

\noindent It is interesting to me how many traditions have noticed that there is something fundamental, or perhaps holy, about trinities.

For this deck, we're focusing on theories that the body or soul has three fundamental parts. It was surprising to me how consistent different systems are at describing the breath as a fundamental part of the body. This is also the origin of the English word ``spirit'', which has the same root as ``respiratory''. The breath is seen as a sort of bridge connecting the animal body to the higher consciousness, though how exactly those two sides are described varies considerably.

The Tria Prima --- sulfur, salt, and mercury --- was already a familiar idea in alchemy before Paracelsus, but he wrote about it extensively in the sixteenth century and made it central to his practice. He understood the three primes as a description of what actually happens to physical substances in the alchemical furnace: quicksilver is that which flows, sulfur is that which burns away, and salt is that which remains behind. But for Paracelsus these were also the three parts of the human being: salt is the body, sulfur the soul, and mercury the spirit. This was not merely a poetic metaphor --- it was a working theory of medicine. A disease of the body called for a salt remedy; an affliction of the soul required sulfur; and a disorder of the spirit demanded mercury. The correspondence between the cosmic and the bodily was, for Paracelsus, the very foundation of healing.

In this deck, I have chosen to call elemental mercury ``quicksilver'' and to use an alternate glyph (\textmercury) to distinguish it from the planet Mercury. This does not match historical usage --- both symbols (and many others!) were used for both concepts, because the two ideas were never fully distinct. The planet and the metal were understood as manifestations of the same principle at different scales. But for the practical purposes of this deck, it helps to have separate names and symbols, such that Quicksilver is the Fool and Mercury is the Magician.

A practice that has become rare in modern astrology that I have revived for this deck is using the symbols for the three primes to also represent the astrological modalities: \textmercury\ mutable, \textsalt\ fixed, \textsulfur\ cardinal. Having a glyph distinct from the planet Mercury ({\symbserif ☿}) makes this less confusing.

The Golden Dawn assigned three Hebrew letters --- the ``mother letters'' of the Sefer Yetzirah --- to the three non-planetary, non-zodiacal major arcana: shin ({\hebrewfont\textdir TRT ש}) to Judgement, mem ({\hebrewfont\textdir TRT מ}) to the Hanged Man, and aleph ({\hebrewfont\textdir TRT א}) to the Fool. Each mother letter carries an elemental association: shin is fire, mem is water, and aleph is air. This maps neatly onto the Tria Prima: sulfur, the soul, has always been associated with fire and combustion; salt, the body, with the waters of dissolution; and mercury, the spirit, with the airy breath.

The threefold division of the person appears independently in several traditions. In Kabbalah, the Zohar (thirteenth-century Spain) elaborates a doctrine of three souls: nefesh, ruach, and neshama. The three treasures of Chinese medicine --- jīng, qì, and shén --- appear in texts as early as the second century BCE, and are central to Daoist internal alchemy. The three doshas of Ayurvedic medicine --- vāta, pitta, and kapha --- are described in the Charaka Samhita, compiled in India also around the second century BCE.
Across these traditions, the quicksilver column is the most consistent. The Kabbalistic ruach, the Chinese qì, and the Ayurvedic vāta are all, at root, the same word: breath. Each tradition independently identified the breath as a fundamental principle --- not merely respiration, but the animating current that moves between body and spirit, between the fixed and the volatile. That so many systems converge on this middle term suggests it may be the easiest of the three to observe directly --- after all, you can feel your own breath right now.

The other two columns are less tidy. Sulfur is the soul, the fiery, active principle --- Paracelsus's ``that which burns away.'' The Kabbalistic neshama is the highest soul, the divine breath closest to God; the Chinese shén is spirit or consciousness; the Ayurvedic pitta is the dosha of fire and transformation. These all share a quality of animating intensity, though they sit at different levels of abstraction. Salt is the body, the fixed, the material remainder --- nefesh is the animal soul bound to the flesh, jīng is the vital essence stored in the body, and kapha is the dosha of earth and water, of structure and cohesion. The pattern holds loosely: one pole burns, the other endures, and the breath moves between them.

Hellenistic astrology uses a system of calculated points called Lots (or Arabic Parts), derived from the positions of planets and the Ascendant. There are sometimes said to be seven, one for each planet, but the three major ones are the Lot of Fortune, the Lot of Spirit, and the Lot of Eros. The Lot of Fortune concerns the body, material circumstances, and physical health. The Lot of Spirit concerns the mind, the soul, intention, and purpose. The Lot of Eros concerns desire, attraction, and the force that draws things together. The correspondence to the three primes suggests itself: the Lot of Spirit maps to sulfur, the active soul; the Lot of Fortune maps to salt, the enduring body; and the Lot of Eros maps to quicksilver, the restless mediating principle that moves between the other two.\footnote{This particular mapping comes from my friend Taalumot.}

I have also included the three dragons from Mahjong tiles, which have a modern association with the three treasures. Mahjong and tarot share a common ancestor, which we will discuss in chapter 38.

\noindent The classical western elements --- Fire, Water, Air, and Earth --- are one of the oldest and most persistent frameworks for understanding the material world. Empedocles proposed them as the four ``roots'' from which everything is composed, and Aristotle refined the idea by associating each element with pairs of qualities: hot or cold, wet or dry.

In the tarot, the four elements correspond directly to the four suits: Wands are Fire, Cups are Water, Swords are Air, and Pentacles are Earth. Each suit thus carries the qualities of its element through all fourteen of its cards.

The elements also map neatly onto the duality system: Fire and Air are active (yang), while Water and Earth are passive (yin). And the Tria Prima cuts across them differently: Sulphur (soul) is associated with Fire, Salt (body) with Earth, and Mercury (spirit) moves between them.

In the Kabbalistic tradition, the four elements are mapped to the four letters of the Tetragrammaton, {\hebrewfont\textdir TRT יהוה} (YHVH): {\hebrewfont\textdir TRT י} (yod) is Fire, the first {\hebrewfont\textdir TRT ה} (heh) is Water, {\hebrewfont\textdir TRT ו} (vav) is Air, and the final {\hebrewfont\textdir TRT ה} (heh) is Earth. This mapping, central to the Golden Dawn system, connects each suit of the tarot to a letter of the divine name.

The Sanskrit names on the facing page are the \textit{Pancha Bhuta}, the five great elements of Hindu philosophy --- here showing only the four that map to Western elements, omitting the fifth, \textit{ākāśa} ({\devfont आकाश}), space or ether. The Golden Dawn adopted this system for their ``tattva vision'' meditations but used the \textit{tanmatra} (subtle quality) names instead: \textit{tejas} (radiance) for fire, \textit{apas} (waters) for water, \textit{vāyu} (wind) for air, and \textit{pṛthvī} (ground) for earth --- drawing from Rama Prasad's 1890 translation \textit{Nature's Finer Forces}.

The directional correspondences on the facing page deserve a note. In both Chinese and Western traditions, south is associated with summer and north with winter, because in the Northern Hemisphere the sun reaches its peak in the southern sky at the summer solstice and is entirely absent from the north. But the Western \textit{astrological} elements seem to contradict this: the hottest season begins with a Water sign (Cancer), and the coldest with an Earth sign (Capricorn). The Chinese system is more internally consistent --- south is summer is fire, north is winter is water. The Western zodiacal elements make more sense if understood as initiating forces rather than descriptions: Aries' fire is the spark that ignites spring's growth, Cancer's water is the nourishment that sustains summer's abundance, Libra's air is the weighing and turning of autumn, and Capricorn's earth is the material hardening against winter's cold.

\begin{center}\fontsize{10}{13}\selectfont\itshape
In the generation of the Five Phases,\\

\begin{flushright}
{\fontsize{8}{10}\selectfont --- Zhōu Dūnyí}

\noindent The Chinese five phases --- Wood ({\cjkfont 木}), Fire ({\cjkfont 火}), Earth ({\cjkfont 土}), Metal ({\cjkfont 金}), and Water ({\cjkfont 水}) --- are not elements in the Greek sense of static building blocks. They are processes, transformations, phases of change that cycle through the natural world.

Each phase has a yin or yang quality. Wood and Fire are yang: expanding, rising, active. Metal and Water are yin: contracting, sinking, receptive. Earth sits at the center, the pivot between them.

The five phases generate and overcome each other in two interlocking cycles. In the generating cycle (\textit{xiāngshēng}), Wood feeds Fire, Fire produces Earth (ash), Earth yields Metal (ore), Metal collects Water (condensation), and Water nourishes Wood. In the overcoming cycle (\textit{xiāngkè}), Wood parts Earth (roots), Earth dams Water, Water quenches Fire, Fire melts Metal, and Metal chops Wood.

On the cards, the Wǔ Xíng appears in the bottom center of each major arcanum, written in Chinese with pinyin transliteration. The phase assigned to each card follows from its planetary or zodiacal association through the traditional Chinese correspondences.
